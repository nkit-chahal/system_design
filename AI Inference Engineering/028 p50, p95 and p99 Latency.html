p50, p95 and p99 Latency

Introduction

A latency number on a dashboard is almost never one number. Every request has its own latency, and together they form a distribution: most requests are fast, a few are slow, and a very few are very slow. A single summary such as the average hides exactly the requests that users complain about. Percentiles describe the distribution instead: the p50 is the latency that half of the requests beat, the p95 the latency that 95% beat, and the p99 the latency that 99% beat.

For LLM serving the distribution is especially lopsided. A request that lands behind a long document, on a replica that just started, or in a moment when the KV cache is full can take ten times longer than a typical one. Figure 1 shows the shape of a typical time-to-first-token distribution and where the average, median and tail percentiles fall on it.

In this lesson, we define percentiles and show why averages mislead, decide which latencies an LLM service must track, explain why tail latency matters more as systems grow, and cover how to measure percentiles correctly: histograms, aggregation across replicas, client-side measurement, and load tests that do not lie. We then turn percentiles into SLOs with error budgets and finish with what usually drives the p99 of an LLM service.

Clarifying the Problem

Percentile questions often start from a dashboard that looks fine while users are unhappy. Here is a typical exchange between a candidate and an interviewer:

Candidate: What are we serving?
Interviewer: Llama 3.1 8B Instruct on vLLM, 8 replicas, each on one H100. Traffic averages 1,000 requests per minute, about 1.44 million a day, and swings between about 8 and 33 requests per second over the day.

Candidate: What does the dashboard show today?
Interviewer: Average time to first token per minute. It sits around 190 ms. But support tickets say that "sometimes the answer takes five seconds to start", and some users say the text freezes mid-answer.

Candidate: Which experience matters most?
Interviewer: How long until the first words appear, and whether the text then streams smoothly.

Candidate: Are there latency targets?
Interviewer: Product wants p95 time to first token at or under 500 ms and p95 time per output token at or under 25 ms, which is at least 40 tokens per second.

Candidate: What measurements do we have?
Interviewer: Prometheus scrapes vLLM's metrics on every replica, and the API gateway logs a timing record for every request.

Candidate: How do we load test?
Interviewer: A script runs 50 simulated users, each sending its next request as soon as the previous one finishes.

The question underneath is: which latencies should we measure, how do we compute their percentiles correctly across 8 replicas, what SLOs should we set, and where do we look when the p99 moves? Each part of the setup above hides a trap, and we will meet all of them.

Percentiles and Why Averages Hide the Tail

What a percentile is

The p-th percentile of a set of latencies is the value at or below which p% of them fall. The simplest definition, the nearest-rank method, sorts the n values and takes the value at position ⌈p×n/100⌉. For 1,000 requests, the p50 is the 500th fastest, the p95 the 950th, and the p99 the 990th. The p50 is the median: a typical request. The p95 and p99 describe the tail: the experience of the unluckiest 5% and 1% of requests. As Google's SRE book puts it, a high-order percentile "shows you a plausible worst-case value" [1].

Averages hide the tail

Suppose 980 of 1,000 requests take 100 ms and 20 take 5 seconds. The mean is (980 × 0.1 + 20 × 5) ÷ 1,000 = 0.198 s, which looks healthy. The p50 and the p95 are both 100 ms. Only the p99, the 990th value, lands in the slow group: 5 seconds. Two percent of users waited 50 times longer than everyone else, and the average barely noticed.

This is exactly our dashboard's problem. Latency distributions are right-skewed, as in Figure 1: the mean is pulled above the median by the tail, but stays far below the tail itself. A 194 ms mean is consistent with a p99 of 1.4 seconds and a maximum of 5 seconds. Worse, the mean moves very little when the tail grows, so it cannot warn us that something is getting worse for the unlucky users.

Interview tip

Never report a latency as a single average. Say "p50, p95 and p99", say which latency (TTFT, time per output token, or end to end), and say over what window and population they were computed.

Each needs its own percentiles, because each answers a different question and has different drivers:

  • TTFT is what users feel as responsiveness. It depends heavily on prompt length, so a fleet serving both chat and 30,000-token documents should look at TTFT percentiles per prompt-length bucket; otherwise the documents' slow prefills define the tail.
  • End-to-end latency grows with the number of output tokens even on a perfectly healthy system: a 2,000-token answer takes four times as long as a 500-token one. Its raw percentiles mostly measure how long answers are, so it makes a poor SLO unless normalized or bucketed by output length.
  • ITL and TPOT look similar but count different things. ITL has one sample per token gap, TPOT one sample per request. Suppose every 500-token response streams at a healthy 20 ms per token but freezes once for 2 seconds. Each request has 499 gaps, one of them 2 s, so stalls are 0.2% of all gaps: the ITL p99 is still 20 ms, and only the p99.9 shows the 2 s. TPOT averages the stall away: (498 × 0.02 + 2) ÷ 499 ≈ 24 ms, which even meets the 25 ms target. Yet every single user saw the text freeze. To catch this, track the worst gap per request, computed at the gateway from the stream, and take its percentiles: here its p99 is 2 s.
  • Queue time is the earliest warning: it rises before TTFT does when the fleet approaches saturation (Lesson 29, Queue Depth).

Why tails matter at scale

If one call has a 1% chance of being slower than its p99, a user action that needs several calls has a much higher chance of hitting at least one slow call. With N independent calls:

P(at least one call slower than its p99)=1−0.99N

Dean and Barroso made this point for web search, where one user query fans out to many servers and the slowest one sets the response time [3]. LLM products have the same structure in a new form. An agent that makes 10 model calls to finish a task hits at least one p99 call 9.6% of the time. A user who has a 20-turn chat sees at least one p99 time to first token in 18.2% of sessions. A pipeline that fans out to 100 calls in parallel waits for a p99 call 63.4% of the time, as Figure 3 shows.

The lesson is that the p99 is not a rare event from the user's point of view. A product used in sessions or agent loops lives in the tail of its model server, which is why serving teams set targets on the p95 and p99, not the median.

Measuring Percentiles Correctly

Histograms and bucket choice

A server cannot keep every latency it has ever seen, so it summarizes them. Prometheus offers two summaries. A summary computes quantiles inside each process; a histogram only counts observations into fixed buckets and lets the query compute quantiles later. Histograms can be added across processes; summaries cannot, because "averaging the quantiles yields statistically nonsensical values" [4]. vLLM exports histograms, which is what we want.

Each bucket is labeled le, short for "less than or equal": it counts every observation at or below its boundary, so the counts are cumulative. To estimate a quantile, Prometheus's histogram_quantile finds the bucket that contains the target rank and assumes observations are spread evenly inside it, which is linear interpolation [5]. Figure 4 works through an example with 10,000 TTFT observations.

The estimate's error is bounded by the width of the bucket it falls in [4], so bucket boundaries matter:

  • In current vLLM, the default TTFT buckets include boundaries at 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0 and 2.5 seconds [6]. Our 500 ms target is a boundary, which makes the question "what fraction of requests met the target?" exact: divide the le="0.5" count by the total.
  • Percentiles that land in a wide bucket are rough. In Figure 4 the p99 lands in the 1 to 2.5 s bucket, so the reported 1.27 s could be off by more than a second. Add boundaries where your percentiles and targets live; vLLM's source shows the defaults can be overridden through its observability configuration [6].
  • Prometheus now recommends native histograms, which use many finer buckets automatically, over both classic histograms and summaries where available [4].

Never average percentiles

The most common mistake on LLM dashboards is to compute a percentile per replica and then average them. Figure 5 shows why this fails. Seven of our eight replicas have a p99 TTFT of 0.3 s. The eighth is in trouble, perhaps freshly started or stuck preempting, and 40% of its requests take about 4 s. The average of the eight p99s is (7 × 0.3 + 4) ÷ 8 ≈ 0.76 s. But the bad replica serves one eighth of the traffic, so 0.4 ÷ 8 = 5% of all requests take about 4 s. That is five times more than the 1% that defines the p99, so the true fleet p99 is 4 s.

The correct method adds the bucket counts across replicas first and computes the quantile once. Prometheus's documentation gives exactly this form [4][5]:

histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(vllm:time_to_first_token_seconds_bucket[5m])))

The rate turns cumulative counters into per-second rates over the window, sum by (le) adds them across every replica while keeping the bucket label that histogram_quantile needs, and the quantile is computed once over the fleet. The same rule applies over time: the p99 for an hour is not the average of the sixty one-minute p99s.

Client-side versus server-side measurement

vLLM's histograms start their clock when a request reaches the engine. They cannot see the time a request spent before that: in the API gateway, in the rate limiter (Lesson 26, Rate Limiting), in the router's queue (Lesson 25, Request Routing), on the network, or in a failed first attempt that the router retried on another replica. The user experiences all of it.

So measure both. The SLO should be measured as close to the user as possible, at the gateway or in the client, because that is the experience we promise. Engine metrics are for diagnosis: they say which replica, and which phase inside it, is slow. If the gateway's p99 TTFT is 2 s while every engine's is 600 ms, the time is being lost outside the engines. Percentiles cannot be subtracted, though, so confirm such a gap with per-request records that carry both timestamps (Lesson 31, Per-Request Token Accounting).

Coordinated omission in load tests

Our load test runs 50 users that each send the next request as soon as the previous one returns. This is a closed-loop test, and it has a subtle flaw called coordinated omission: when the server stalls, the load generator stalls with it and stops sending, so the requests that real users would have sent during the stall are never sent and never measured. Figure 6 shows a 2-second stall.

The closed loop records one slow sample out of eight, so even its p80 looks healthy. Real users arrive on their own schedule, not when the server is ready, so in production the stall would have delayed four requests out of twelve. The effect can be enormous. The wrk2 load generator's documentation shows a test with a 1.4-second server pause where the uncorrected p99 was 6.04 ms and the corrected p99 was 1.27 s, about 200 times larger [7]. The fix is an open-loop test: send requests on a fixed or random arrival schedule regardless of responses, and measure each latency from the time the request should have been sent. vLLM's benchmark does this with vllm bench serve --request-rate, which generates Poisson arrivals by default [8]. Its --max-concurrency option caps requests in flight on the client side, which reintroduces a queue the server metrics do not see, so use it with care in SLO tests.

SLOs and Error Budgets for LLM Latency

Writing the SLO

A service level indicator (SLI) is a measured quantity, and a service level objective (SLO) is a target for it [1]. Latency SLOs are most useful when written as the fraction of requests that are fast enough, measured over a window, because fractions can be counted exactly and added up over time. For our service:

  • TTFT: at least 95% of requests with prompts up to 8,000 tokens get their first token within 500 ms, measured at the gateway over a rolling 30 days. This is the same as "p95 TTFT ≤ 500 ms", but stated so it can be counted. Document requests get a separate, looser SLO, since a 30,000-token prompt alone needs about 1 s of prefill.
  • Streaming: at least 95% of requests have a TPOT of 25 ms or less, and at least 99% have no gap between tokens longer than 1 s. The second clause catches the freezes that TPOT averages away.
  • Guardrail: p99 TTFT under 2 s, watched on the dashboard but not promised to customers.

Excluding requests also needs care: requests rejected by rate limits (429) do not count as slow, but requests the fleet sheds under overload (503) should count against an availability SLO, or shedding becomes a way to hide latency problems.

A worked error budget

An SLO of 95% leaves an error budget of 5%: the share of requests allowed to be slow. At 1,000 requests per minute, the service handles about 1.44 million requests per day and 43.2 million per 30 days, so the TTFT budget is 0.05 × 43.2 million = 2.16 million slow requests per month.

Suppose a bad rollout restarts replicas during the daily peak, and for 2 hours 40% of requests miss 500 ms. At 33 requests per second, 2 hours is about 237,600 requests, so the incident produces about 95,000 slow requests, 4.4% of the monthly budget. The burn rate, how fast the budget is consumed relative to plan, is 0.40 ÷ 0.05 = 8: at that rate the 30-day budget would last 30 ÷ 8 = 3.75 days. The budget also exposes chronic problems. If 3% of requests are slow on a normal day, that alone uses 3 ÷ 5 = 60% of the budget, leaving little room for incidents, and is a reason to fix the baseline before shipping features.

Alerting on burn rate

Alerting on a raw p99 is noisy, especially when traffic is low. At the daily peak of 33 requests per second, a 5-minute window holds about 9,900 requests, so the p99 rests on the slowest 99 and the p99.9 on about 10. At the overnight low of 8 requests per second, the same window holds 2,400 requests and the p99 rests on 24: a few slow requests swing it.

Alerting on burn rate avoids this. Google's SRE workbook recommends multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts: for example, page when the burn rate over the last hour exceeds 14.4, which spends 2% of a 30-day budget in that hour, confirmed over the last 5 minutes; page when it exceeds 6 over 6 hours, spending 5%; and open a ticket when it exceeds 1 over 3 days [9]. The arithmetic works for any target: a burn rate b sustained for h hours spends b×h÷720 of a 30-day budget. The workbook's examples assume a 99.9% target; with our 5% budget the highest possible burn rate is 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20, and 14.4 would mean 72% of requests are slow, so the 6-hour threshold of 6 (30% slow) is the more useful page for this SLO.

Which percentiles and SLOs fit our deployment?

We replace the average on the dashboard with p50, p95 and p99 of TTFT, computed from fleet-wide bucket sums, plus the same per replica to spot a bad one, and split TTFT by prompt length. For streaming we track p95 TPOT and the p99 of the worst gap per request, measured at the gateway. Queue time p99 goes on the first row because it moves first. The SLOs are the ones above: 95% of chat requests under 500 ms TTFT at the gateway, 95% at or under 25 ms TPOT, and 99% with no gap over 1 s, each with burn-rate alerts. We keep the p99 as a diagnostic rather than a promise because at night it rests on a handful of requests, and we fix the load test to open loop so its p99 can be trusted. The support tickets match this picture: a 190 ms average with a tail of multi-second waits and mid-answer freezes.

What Drives p99 in LLM Serving

Queueing near saturation

The biggest driver of tail latency in any service is queueing, and it grows much faster than load. In the simplest queueing model, M/M/1, with service time S and utilization ρ, the time a request spends in the system is exponentially distributed with mean S/(1−ρ), so its p99 is ln(100)≈4.6 times the mean. Figure 7 plots both for a 100 ms service time.

LLM engines batch requests, so their numbers differ, but the shape holds: the tail grows like 1/(1−ρ), so the last 10% of utilization costs far more latency than the first 50%. This is why a fleet sized to run at its daily peak of 33 requests per second against a capacity of 40, about 83% utilization, has little room before its p99 TTFT breaks, and why autoscaling (Lesson 22, Autoscaling) and load shedding (Lesson 26) act on queue signals before utilization reaches 100%.

Long prompts

A 30,000-token prompt needs about 1 s of prefill on an H100, assuming about 30,800 prefill tokens per second (Lesson 25, Request Routing, derives this rough figure). That request misses a 500 ms target on its own, which is why its SLO is separate. It also delays every request queued behind it. Lesson 36, Long-Context Serving, covers prompts far longer than this.

Prefill interference

With continuous batching (Lesson 7, Continuous Batching), a new request's prefill runs in the same engine steps as the decode steps of streams already running. If a 30,000-token prompt is prefilled in one step, every running stream waits about 1 s for its next token, a spike in their ITL and worst gap. Chunked prefill splits the prompt across many steps so decodes keep flowing; Sarathi-Serve showed that chunked prefills with stall-free scheduling add new requests "without pausing ongoing decodes" [10]. In vLLM, --max-num-batched-tokens caps the tokens processed in one iteration [11]. Figure 8 compares the two.

The trade is explicit: the running streams' worst gap falls from about 1 s to about 86 ms, while the long prompt's own TTFT rises from about 1.0 s to about 1.3 s. Disaggregated serving goes further by running prefill and decode on separate GPUs, which removes the interference entirely [12]; Lesson 34, Disaggregated Prefill and Decode, covers it.

Preemption

When the KV cache is full, vLLM preempts running requests and later recomputes their caches (Lesson 3, KV Cache). The preempted requests see a long stall, and recomputation takes GPU time from everyone else. vLLM counts these events in vllm:num_preemptions and reports KV cache usage in vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc [2]. Preemptions that rise with load are a sign that the replica admits more concurrent sequences than its cache can hold.

Cold replicas and cache misses

A replica that just started has an empty prefix cache (Lesson 24, Prefix Caching) and may still be warming up (Lesson 23, Cold Starts), so its first requests run full prefills. If the router sends it a full share of traffic immediately, its requests populate the fleet's tail, which is exactly the "one bad replica" of Figure 5. Per-replica p99 TTFT, read together with the prefix cache hit rate from vllm:prefix_cache_hits and vllm:prefix_cache_queries [2], identifies it.

import math
import random


def percentile(values, p):
    """Nearest-rank percentile: the smallest value with at least p% of values at or below it."""
    ordered = sorted(values)
    return ordered[max(0, math.ceil(p / 100 * len(ordered)) - 1)]

      f"p95 {percentile(ttft, 95)} s, p99 {percentile(ttft, 99)} s")

# 2. Never average percentiles: 7 healthy replicas and 1 bad one, equal traffic.
random.seed(0)
healthy = lambda: random.uniform(0.05, 0.3)
replicas = [[healthy() for _ in range(1_000)] for _ in range(7)]
replicas.append([4.0 if random.random() < 0.4 else healthy() for _ in range(1_000)])
avg_of_p99s = sum(percentile(r, 99) for r in replicas) / len(replicas)
fleet_p99 = percentile([x for r in replicas for x in r], 99)
print(f"average of per-replica p99s {avg_of_p99s:.2f} s, true fleet p99 {fleet_p99:.2f} s")

Other Talking Points

If there is extra time at the end of the interview, here are some additional talking points:

  • Goodput: the rate of requests that meet every latency target, rather than raw throughput; vllm bench serve --goodput takes SLO thresholds for TTFT, TPOT and end-to-end latency and reports it [8].
  • Hedged requests reduce tail latency by sending a second copy after a delay [3], but for LLMs each copy repeats the prefill, so they suit only short requests (Lesson 25, Request Routing).
  • Normalizing end-to-end latency by output length, for example milliseconds per output token, so that long answers do not look like slow service.
  • Per-tenant percentiles: one tenant with very long prompts can own the fleet's tail; slicing by tenant shows whether an SLO miss is everyone's or one customer's (Lesson 31, Per-Request Token Accounting).
  • Choosing windows: short windows for alerting, long windows for SLO compliance, and longer windows at night when traffic is low.
  • Correlating latency percentiles with GPU and queue metrics on one dashboard, the subject of Lesson 32, Lab - A Dashboard That Explains Latency Spikes.

Summary

  • Latency is a distribution. Report p50, p95 and p99; a mean of 194 ms can hide a p99 of 1.4 s and a maximum of 5 s.
  • Track TTFT, time per output token, inter-token latency, queue time and end-to-end latency separately, and add the worst gap per request: a 2 s freeze in every answer leaves the ITL p99 at 20 ms and the TPOT at 24 ms.
  • Tails compound: with N calls, the chance of hitting at least one p99 is 1 − 0.99N, 18.2% for a 20-turn chat and 63.4% for 100 calls.
  • Compute percentiles from histograms with histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(..._bucket[5m]))). Never average percentiles across replicas or time windows, put bucket boundaries at your targets, and measure the SLO at the gateway.
  • Load test open loop, with arrivals on a schedule. Closed-loop tests suffer coordinated omission and can understate the p99 by orders of magnitude.
  • Write latency SLOs as fractions of fast requests and alert on burn rate: 2 hours with 40% slow requests spends about 4.4% of a month's 5% budget. The p99 is driven by queueing near saturation, long prompts, prefill interference, preemption and cold replicas.
