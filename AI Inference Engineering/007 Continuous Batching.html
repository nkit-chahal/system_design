Continuous Batching

Introduction

A GPU that serves one user at a time spends most of its time waiting on memory. Every decode step streams all of the model's weights out of memory to produce a single token. If the same step serves 64 users, the weights are read once and 64 tokens come out. Batching is how a serving engine turns this memory-bound loop into a high-throughput one.

LLM requests are awkward to batch, though. Their prompts have different lengths, nobody knows how long each answer will be, and they arrive at random times. A server that forms a batch, runs it until every request is done, and only then takes the next batch leaves slots empty as short answers finish and makes new arrivals wait for the slowest request. Continuous batching fixes this by rebuilding the batch at every forward pass: finished requests leave and waiting requests join between one step and the next. The same idea is called iteration-level scheduling in research papers and in-flight batching in TensorRT-LLM.

In this lesson, we look at why batching matters so much for decode, what static batching wastes, how iteration-level scheduling works, how a modern scheduler decides what goes into each step, and how to tune it for a latency target.

Clarifying the Problem

Batching questions usually start from a slow or expensive server. Here is a typical exchange between a candidate and an interviewer:

Candidate: Which model and hardware are we serving on?
Interviewer: Llama 3.1 8B Instruct in BF16 on NVIDIA H100 80 GB GPUs. The fleet averages 1,000 requests per minute, but today we are tuning a single H100 replica.

Candidate: What does the traffic look like?
Interviewer: Mostly chat. Prompts are about 2,000 tokens and answers average 500 tokens, but answers range from a few dozen tokens to about 1,000. Arrivals are bursty, and a few users paste 32K-token documents.

Candidate: What are the latency targets?
Interviewer: p95 time to first token (TTFT) of at most 500 ms, and p95 time per output token (TPOT) of at most 25 ms, so every stream gets at least 40 tokens per second.

Candidate: How is the current server batching requests?
Interviewer: It collects up to 64 requests, runs them to completion, then takes the next 64. Throughput is poor, and users wait several seconds before the first token appears.

Candidate: Can we change the serving engine and its settings, or only the hardware?
Interviewer: We can move to vLLM and tune it. Adding replicas is a separate discussion.

The question underneath is: how do we keep the GPU's batch full at every step without letting any single user's latency blow up? To answer it, we first need to see how much a full batch is worth.

Why Batching Matters for Decode

One weight read, many tokens

As Lesson 2, Prefill vs Decode, showed, a decode step does very little arithmetic per byte it reads. Llama 3.1 8B has about 16.06 GB of BF16 weights, and the H100 reads memory at 3.35 TB/s, so just streaming the weights takes about 4.8 ms. That cost is paid once per step, no matter how many sequences the step serves. Each extra sequence adds only the cost of reading its own KV cache and a small amount of compute.

For a batch of B sequences with an average context of c tokens, the step time has two lower bounds, one from memory and one from compute:

tstep≥max(W+B·c·skvBW, 2N·BF)

Here W is the bytes of weights, skv is 128 KiB of KV cache per token (Lesson 3, KV Cache), BW is memory bandwidth, N is the number of parameters, and F is peak compute (about 989 TFLOPS dense BF16). Total throughput is B/tstep tokens per second, and each user receives 1/tstep.

Figure 2 applies the formula to our chat traffic, where a request in the middle of its answer holds about 2,250 tokens of context, or about 295 MB of cache:

  • Batch 1: 16.06 GB + 0.3 GB read per step ⇒ at least 4.9 ms ⇒ about 205 tokens per second in total.
  • Batch 64: 16.06 GB + 18.9 GB ⇒ at least 10.4 ms ⇒ about 6,140 tokens per second, 96 per user.
  • Batch 170 (the cache limit from Lesson 3): 16.06 GB + 50.1 GB ⇒ at least 19.8 ms ⇒ about 8,600 tokens per second, 51 per user.

Going from 1 to 170 sequences multiplies throughput by about 42 while each user's speed drops by only about 4×. Compute stays far below its limit the whole time: at 989 TFLOPS against 3.35 TB/s, a step needs roughly 295 tokens before multiplying by the weights takes as long as reading them. This is the central trade-off of batching, and every setting in this lesson moves us along this curve.

Static Batching and Its Waste

How request-level batching works

Classic model serving batches requests that look alike: an image classifier's requests share one shape and finish in one forward pass. An LLM request has a variable prompt length, an output length that is unknown until the end-of-sequence token, and hundreds of forward passes. In static batching, the server collects a group of requests, pads their prompts to a common length, runs prefill, and then runs decode steps until every request in the group has finished. Only then does it return the results and form the next group. Dynamic batching, as offered by general-purpose servers such as NVIDIA Triton, is the same scheme with a smarter trigger: it waits until either the batch is full or a short timeout expires. Both schedule at the granularity of a whole request, so we call them request-level batching.

Three kinds of waste

Figure 3 shows the three costs of request-level batching:

  1. Padding. A batch tensor needs one length, so a 600-token prompt is padded to the 2,000 tokens of its longest neighbor. The engine computes and stores padding that no one will read.
  2. Waiting for the longest sequence. A request that finishes early keeps its slot until the longest request in the batch is done. Suppose eight requests produce 120, 250, 300, 400, 500, 600, 830, and 1,000 tokens. They need 4,000 useful token-steps, but the batch runs for 1,000 steps with 8 slots, which is 8,000 slot-steps. Half of the decode work is wasted, and the waste grows with the spread of output lengths.
  3. Head-of-line blocking. A request that arrives just after a batch starts must wait for that whole batch. At 1,000 steps of about 10.4 ms, that is about 10.4 seconds before its prefill can even begin, and about 5.2 seconds on average.

The Orca paper calls the second and third problems early-finished and late-joining requests [1]. Reserving KV cache memory for each request's maximum length adds a fourth, memory-side waste, which is the subject of Lesson 8, PagedAttention.

Iteration-Level Scheduling

The Orca idea

Orca, presented at OSDI 2022, proposed scheduling at the granularity of one model iteration instead of one request [1]. Its scheduler repeats three steps: pick the requests to run next, ask the engine to run one forward pass on them, and collect one new token per request. Because control returns to the scheduler after every pass, a finished request leaves the batch at once and a new request can start at the next pass. On GPT-3 175B, the authors report 36.9× higher throughput than NVIDIA FasterTransformer at the same latency.

Selective batching

Iteration-level scheduling creates a shape problem. In a single pass, one request may be processing a 2,000-token prompt while another is generating its 300th token. Their inputs cannot be stacked into the usual [B,L,H] tensor, because L differs per request.

Orca's answer is selective batching. Most of a transformer layer, such as the linear projections, the MLP, and layer normalization, treats every token independently, so Orca flattens all tokens of the step into one [∑L,H] tensor and runs those operations on it as a single batch. Only attention needs to know which tokens belong to which request, so the tensor is split per request for attention and merged again afterwards. Figure 4 shows both halves of the idea.

This is why modern engines think in tokens per step rather than requests per batch. vLLM, SGLang, and TensorRT-LLM all build on this model; TensorRT-LLM's documentation describes its in-flight batching as packed sequences with no padding, where context-phase and generation-phase sequences run in the same step [2].

What Orca did not fix

Orca still reserved KV cache space for each request's maximum possible length when it admitted the request. Iteration-level scheduling filled the compute side of the batch; PagedAttention later filled the memory side. Together they give the "continuous batching" that engines ship today [3].

How a Modern Scheduler Works

Waiting and running queues

A continuous-batching scheduler keeps two queues. The waiting queue holds requests that have arrived but have no KV cache yet. The running queue holds requests that own KV cache blocks and are being prefilled or decoded. Before every forward pass, the scheduler decides how many tokens each running request gets, which waiting requests to admit, and whether anyone must be evicted to make room.

vLLM's scheduler does not even keep separate prefill and decode phases. Each request simply tracks how many of its tokens have been computed so far, and each step assigns tokens so that this count catches up with the request's length [4]. A request in prefill is behind by many tokens; a request in decode is behind by one.

The per-step token budget and the sequence cap

Two limits shape every step:

  • The token budget caps the total number of tokens in one forward pass. In vLLM it is --max-num-batched-tokens, documented as the "maximum number of tokens that can be processed in a single iteration" [5].
  • The sequence cap limits how many requests may be running at once. In vLLM it is --max-num-seqs, the "maximum number of sequences to be processed in a single iteration" [5].

Their defaults depend on the hardware and on how vLLM is started. In vLLM 0.32, vllm serve on an H100 or H200 defaults to 8,192 tokens and 1,024 sequences; on an A100 or a GPU with less than 70 GiB of memory, it uses 2,048 tokens and 256 sequences; offline use through the Python LLM class gets a larger token budget of 16,384 on H100-class GPUs [6]. These values have changed between releases, so check the startup log of the version you run. TensorRT-LLM's trtllm-serve exposes the same two limits as --max_num_tokens and --max_batch_size. SGLang's closest equivalents are --chunked-prefill-size, the largest prefill chunk, and --max-running-requests; by default SGLang runs prefill and decode in separate batches, and --enable-mixed-chunk lets them share one [7].

One scheduling step

Figure 5 shows how one step is assembled. In vLLM's current scheduler, the order is [4]:

  1. Running requests first. Each running request gets the tokens it still needs: one token if it is decoding, or the next chunk of its prompt if it is partway through prefill, as long as budget remains. If the KV cache has no free block for a request's new tokens, the scheduler preempts the most recently admitted running request (or the lowest-priority one under the priority policy) and tries again.
  2. Then waiting requests. If nobody was preempted in this step, the scheduler admits waiting requests in order while budget remains, the running count stays below the sequence cap, and the KV cache has room. A prompt longer than the remaining budget is admitted with only its first chunk.
  3. One forward pass runs over all scheduled tokens, flattened as in selective batching.
  4. Bookkeeping. New tokens are sampled and streamed, finished requests leave the running queue, and their KV blocks return to the free pool.

With the H100 default budget of 8,192 tokens and 128 requests decoding, the decodes use 128 tokens. Four new 2,000-token prompts take 8,000 of the remaining 8,064, and a fifth prompt starts with a 64-token chunk that continues in the next step. Decode and prefill share one forward pass.

Mixing prefill and decode: chunked prefill

Mixing has a cost: a step is only as fast as its slowest part. A decode-only step with 64 sequences takes at least 10.4 ms on our H100. Adding prefill tokens adds compute, about 16 GFLOP per token for an 8B model, and a large prefill makes the step compute-bound. A 32K-token document processed in one piece needs at least 532 ms of compute. If the engine ran it in a single step, every one of the 64 users would see their stream freeze for more than half a second.

Chunked prefill splits long prompts into pieces that fit the token budget and runs each piece alongside the ongoing decodes. The SARATHI paper introduced chunked prefills and decode-maximal batching, where each batch carries one prefill chunk and fills the rest with decodes [8]. Sarathi-Serve added stall-free scheduling, which admits new requests without pausing ongoing decodes, and reported 2.6× more serving capacity than vLLM for Mistral-7B on one A100 under tail-latency limits [9]. vLLM now enables chunked prefill by default and schedules all pending decodes before any prefill [10].

Chunking does not reduce the total prefill work; the 32K document needs 532 ms of compute either way. It changes who waits. Two lessons come out of the table. First, above about 1,000 tokens the document's prefill time barely changes, but every other user's worst-case gap between tokens keeps growing, from 17 ms to 133 ms. Second, a small budget is not free either: at 512 tokens the step is limited by memory, compute sits partly idle, and the document's prefill stretches to 0.76 s. No budget gets that prefill under 532 ms, the compute it needs on one H100.

The sweet spot sits where a step's compute time equals the memory time it spends anyway on the decodes. We can call this the balance point:

tokens*=F·tmem2N

With 64 decodes, tmem is about 10.4 ms, so the balance point is about 640 tokens per step. Below it, prefill tokens ride along with decodes almost for free; above it, every extra prefill token lengthens the step for all users. With 128 decodes the balance point is about 990 tokens, and with 170 it is about 1,220. Real engines also pay a fixed overhead per step for scheduling and kernel launches, which this model ignores and which favors somewhat larger steps. That is why vLLM's tuning guide recommends values such as 2,048 for good inter-token latency, larger values for better time to first token, and values above 8,192 for throughput-oriented offline work [10].

One more knob handles a single long prompt hogging the budget. vLLM's --long-prefill-token-threshold caps how many tokens one long prompt may take per step, so several prompts can prefill side by side; its default of 0 disables the cap [5]. Prompts of 128K tokens and more raise further issues, covered in Lesson 36, Long-Context Serving.

Admission, the KV cache, and preemption

The token budget limits compute per step; the KV cache limits how many requests can be in flight at all. Recent vLLM versions admit a waiting request only if its whole prompt fits in the free KV blocks, not just its first chunk, so a long prompt does not start and then stall halfway [4]. It does not reserve space for future output tokens, since that is exactly the waste PagedAttention removes, so the cache can run out while requests are generating.

When a running request needs a new block and none is free, the scheduler preempts another request: it frees all of that request's blocks at once and puts it back at the front of the waiting queue. vLLM's default recovery is recomputation: when the request is readmitted, its prompt and the tokens it has already generated are prefilled again as one long prompt [10]. Lesson 3, KV Cache, introduced this mechanism, and Figure 7 places it in a request's life; here what matters is its cost. Preempting a chat request that holds 2,000 prompt tokens and 400 generated tokens throws away 2,400 tokens of cache, which costs at least 39 ms of compute to rebuild, plus however long the request waits to be readmitted.

Occasional preemption under a burst is normal; frequent preemption means the server admits more work than its cache can hold. It shows up as a rising vllm:num_preemptions counter (scraped as vllm:num_preemptions_total), vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc pinned near 1, and latency spikes for the preempted users [11]. The vLLM tuning guide lists the fixes: give the cache more memory with --gpu-memory-utilization, lower --max-num-seqs or --max-num-batched-tokens, or spread the model over more GPUs with tensor or pipeline parallelism [10].

Tuning Continuous Batching

Worked example: what continuous batching buys us

Now we compare the interviewer's prototype with continuous batching on the same H100, using the lower bounds from earlier and assuming, as in the eight-request example, that the longest answer in a batch is about twice the 500-token average.

The prototype (static, 64 requests). Prefilling 64 prompts of 2,000 tokens is 128,000 tokens, at least 2.08 s of compute, during which no one decodes. Decoding then runs 1,000 steps of about 10.4 ms, or 10.4 s, until the longest answer finishes. The cycle takes about 12.5 s and delivers 64 × 500 = 32,000 tokens, which is about 2,560 tokens per second. A request that arrives mid-cycle waits about 6 s on average before its prefill starts.

Continuous batching with 64 slots, ideal case. In steady state a request finishes every 500 ÷ 64 ≈ 7.8 steps, so about 256 new prompt tokens arrive per step. If they are spread evenly, a step carries 64 decode tokens and 256 prompt tokens, which needs about 5.2 ms of compute, less than the 10.4 ms the step already spends reading memory. Prefill rides along for free, and throughput approaches 6,140 tokens per second, about 2.4× the prototype. A new request waits one step, not one batch.

What a greedy scheduler actually gets. The scheduler does not spread prompts evenly. With a 2,048-token budget, it prefills each new 2,000-token prompt in a single compute-bound step of at least 33 ms, and the decodes in that step wait for it. The simulator in the In Practice section follows vLLM's step order with the same lower-bound costs. It gives about 4,680 tokens per second for 64 slots at a 2,048-token budget (1.8× the prototype) and about 5,910 at a 512-token budget, close to the ideal because 512 is near the 640-token balance point for 64 decodes.

More slots, budget near the balance point. With 128 slots and a 1,024-token budget, the simulator reaches about 7,490 tokens per second (2.9×), against an ideal of about 7,970. With 170 slots, the cache limit, it reaches about 8,130 (3.2×) against an ideal of about 8,600. Each step up costs per-user speed: decode-only steps give about 96 tokens per second per user at 64 slots, 62 at 128, and 51 at 170.

Real engines reach only part of these bounds, so treat Figure 8 as the shape of the answer, not a benchmark. Published measurements show the same shape: on OPT-13B on one A100, an Anyscale study measured about 8× higher throughput from continuous batching alone over naive static batching, and up to 23× when combined with vLLM's memory management [12].

Interview tip

Explain the gain in two parts. Continuous batching removes idle slots, which is worth about 2× when the longest answer is twice the average. It also lets prefill ride inside memory-bound decode steps, as long as the token budget keeps steps near the balance point. Then say what it costs: a bigger running batch means slower tokens for each user.

Which settings fit our deployment?

Our targets are p95 TTFT of at most 500 ms and p95 TPOT of at most 25 ms. The TPOT target, not memory, sets the batch size: the cache could hold about 170 chat requests, but Lesson 19, GPU Utilization, puts a real decode step at about 21 ms with 53 streams, roughly twice our lower bound, which leaves little room above that. We keep continuous batching with chunked prefill, which vLLM turns on by default, and change two settings:

  • Sequence cap of 64 instead of 1,024. It sits a little above the 53 streams one H100 carries at about 5 requests per second, so short bursts are absorbed without pushing steps far past 25 ms. At 64 requests the cache holds at most 64 × 2,500 = 160,000 tokens, 38% of its 426,000, so preemption should almost never happen.
  • Token budget of 512 instead of the H100 default of 8,192. That is just under the 640-token balance point for 64 decodes, so even a step that carries a prefill chunk stays memory-bound at about 10.4 ms in the lower-bound model, and prefill adds almost nothing to TPOT. A 2,000-token chat prompt is prefilled in 5 steps, about 52 ms, far inside the 500 ms TTFT target, and the simulator gives about 5,910 tokens per second, 2.3× the prototype. With 8,192, any step carrying a document chunk takes at least 133 ms, which would hurt the TPOT of everyone in the batch.

The 32K documents are the exception. Their prefill needs at least 532 ms of compute, so they cannot meet a 500 ms TTFT on one H100 with any budget; at 512 tokens they take about 0.76 s. The p95 target still holds while documents stay a small share of requests. If that share grows, they need more GPUs per request or a separate pool (Lesson 36, Long-Context Serving).

The model ignores per-step overheads, so these settings are a starting point: the load test (Lesson 12) compares budgets of 512, 1,024, and 2,048, keeps the largest that holds p95 TPOT under 25 ms, then tries a slightly higher cap. At about 5 requests per second per H100, the fleet's average of 16.7 requests per second needs at least four replicas, more at the daily peak; Lesson 22, Autoscaling, covers that side. If bursts push TTFT over 500 ms, the answer is more replicas, not a bigger batch.

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass

# Lower-bound model of Llama 3.1 8B (BF16) on one H100, as in this lesson.
WEIGHT_BYTES = 16.06e9
KV_BYTES_PER_TOKEN = 131_072
BANDWIDTH = 3.35e12          # bytes per second
PEAK_FLOPS = 989e12          # dense BF16
FLOPS_PER_TOKEN = 2 * 8.03e9


def step_time(context_tokens: int, new_tokens: int) -> float:
    """One forward pass: the slower of memory traffic and compute."""
    memory = (WEIGHT_BYTES + KV_BYTES_PER_TOKEN * context_tokens) / BANDWIDTH
    compute = FLOPS_PER_TOKEN * new_tokens / PEAK_FLOPS
    return max(memory, compute)


@dataclass
class Request:
    prompt: int
    output: int
    computed: int = 0    # prompt tokens already prefilled
    generated: int = 0   # output tokens produced so far


def make_requests(n: int, seed: int = 0) -> list[tuple[int, int]]:
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return [(2000, rng.randint(50, 950)) for _ in range(n)]  # 500-token mean


def run_static(requests, batch_size):
    clock = 0.0
    for i in range(0, len(requests), batch_size):
        batch = requests[i:i + batch_size]
        clock += step_time(0, sum(p for p, _ in batch))   # one padded prefill
        longest = max(o for _, o in batch)
        for step in range(1, longest):
            # Finished requests keep their slot until the longest one is done.
            clock += step_time(sum(p + step for p, _ in batch), len(batch))
    return sum(o for _, o in requests) / clock


def run_continuous(requests, max_seqs, budget):
    waiting = [Request(p, o) for p, o in requests]
    running, clock = [], 0.0
    while waiting or running:
        left, plan = budget, []
        for r in running:                       # 1. running requests first
            if left == 0:
                break
            n = 1 if r.computed == r.prompt else min(r.prompt - r.computed, left)
            plan.append((r, n))
            left -= n
        while waiting and left > 0 and len(running) < max_seqs:   # 2. admit
            r = waiting.pop(0)
            n = min(r.prompt, left)
            running.append(r)
            plan.append((r, n))
            left -= n
        context = sum(r.computed + r.generated for r, _ in plan)
        clock += step_time(context, sum(n for _, n in plan))
        for r, n in plan:
            if r.computed < r.prompt:
                r.computed += n
                if r.computed == r.prompt:
                    r.generated += 1            # the last chunk samples a token
            else:
                r.generated += 1
        running = [r for r in running if r.generated < r.output]
    return sum(o for _, o in requests) / clock


reqs = make_requests(3000)
print(f"static, 64 slots:                {run_static(reqs, 64):6.0f} tokens/s")
for slots, budget in [(64, 2048), (64, 512), (128, 1024), (128, 2048), (170, 1024)]:
    tput = run_continuous(reqs, slots, budget)
    print(f"continuous, {slots:3d} slots, {budget:4d} budget: {tput:6.0f} tokens/s")

def scrape() -> dict[str, float]:
    text = urllib.request.urlopen(URL, timeout=5).read().decode()
    values: dict[str, float] = {}
    for line in text.splitlines():
        if line.startswith("#") or not line.strip():
            continue
        name = re.split(r"[{ ]", line, maxsplit=1)[0]
        if name in WANTED:
            values[name] = values.get(name, 0.0) + float(line.rsplit(" ", 1)[1])
    return values


last_preemptions = None
while True:

    preemptions = m.get("vllm:num_preemptions_total", 0.0)
    new = 0 if last_preemptions is None else preemptions - last_preemptions
    last_preemptions = preemptions
    print(f"running={m.get('vllm:num_requests_running', 0):4.0f}  "

Other Talking Points

If there is extra time at the end of the interview, here are some additional talking points:

  • Disaggregated serving runs prefill and decode on different GPUs, so long prompts can never slow down token streaming; DistServe is one well-known design [13], and Lesson 34, Disaggregated Prefill and Decode, covers the approach.
  • Priority scheduling: with --scheduling-policy priority, vLLM orders requests by a per-request priority and preempts the lowest-priority running request first, which protects interactive users during batch-job bursts [5] (Lesson 39, Batch and Offline Inference).
  • SGLang calls preemption "retracting" a request, and its --schedule-conservativeness setting makes admission more cautious when retractions are frequent [7].
  • Speculative decoding adds several draft tokens per request to each step, so it consumes token budget that would otherwise go to prefill (Lesson 15, Speculative Decoding).
  • The vllm:iteration_tokens_total histogram shows how many tokens each step actually carried, which tells you whether the token budget or the sequence cap is the binding limit [11].
  • Continuous batching raises throughput but cannot create capacity: when arrivals exceed what the GPU can process, the waiting queue grows without bound, and the fix is admission control or more replicas (Lesson 26, Rate Limiting).

Summary

  • Decode is memory-bound, so one weight read serves many sequences: on one H100, Llama 3.1 8B goes from about 205 tokens per second at batch 1 to about 8,600 at batch 170, while each user drops from 205 to 51.
  • Request-level batching wastes slots on padding and finished requests and makes arrivals wait for a whole batch; with the longest answer twice the average, half the decode work is wasted.
  • Orca's iteration-level scheduling rebuilds the batch at every forward pass, and selective batching flattens all tokens of a step into one tensor so that prompts and decode tokens can share a pass.
  • Modern schedulers run requests in decode first, then admit waiting requests, within a per-step token budget (--max-num-batched-tokens) and a running-sequence cap (--max-num-seqs); in vLLM 0.32 these default to 8,192 and 1,024 for vllm serve on an H100.
  • Chunked prefill spreads long prompts across steps without reducing prefill work. With 64 decodes, a 2,048-token budget bounds steps near 33 ms and 8,192 lets them reach 133 ms; the balance point, where compute time equals memory time, is about 640 tokens.
  • When the KV cache runs out, the scheduler preempts and later recomputes requests; frequent preemption means too much admitted work.
  • For our 25 ms TPOT target we choose a 64-request cap and a 512-token budget, about 2.3× the throughput of the static prototype in the lower-bound model, then tune against measured p95 latency.
