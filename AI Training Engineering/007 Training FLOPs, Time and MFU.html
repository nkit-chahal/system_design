Training FLOPs, Time and MFU

Introduction

Training large language models is governed by rigid arithmetic. Before launching a distributed cluster of hundreds or thousands of GPUs, an infrastructure engineer must answer three fundamental questions: How many floating-point operations will the training run take? How many days will the job monopolize the cluster? And how much will the compute bill cost? Answering these questions requires modeling the relationship between model architecture, dataset volume, hardware silicon limits, and cluster efficiency [1].

The standard industry baseline for transformer compute is the 6ND rule: training a dense autoregressive language model with N parameters on D tokens requires approximately 6ND floating-point operations (FLOPs) [2]. When teams apply full activation recomputation to fit large micro-batches into GPU High Bandwidth Memory, that cost increases by 33% to 8ND [3]. For modern long-context regimes, the quadratic attention operation adds an additional layer-dependent term that can alter the compute budget significantly [4].

However, theoretical FLOPs tell only half the story. Physical accelerators never operate at 100% of their advertised peak throughput. Inter-GPU communication, memory bandwidth limits, pipeline bubbles, and kernel launch overhead inevitably degrade performance. To measure real-world productivity, the field relies on Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU)—the ratio of theoretical minimal compute delivered to peak theoretical hardware capacity over wall-clock time [1]. Unlike Hardware FLOPs Utilization (HFU), which artificially rewards redundant recomputation, MFU is implementation-independent and serves as the definitive engineering metric of cluster productivity.

In this lesson, we derive the 6ND and 8ND rules from first principles, formulate the quadratic attention term for long sequences, dissect the formal definitions of MFU and HFU, analyze why frontier training runs achieve 38% to 45% MFU, translate abstract efficiency into daily tokens-per-second telemetry, and calculate the concrete financial and operational penalty of underperforming clusters.

Clarifying the Problem

In system design interviews and cluster capacity reviews, training sizing questions begin by constraining the model, dataset, and target hardware. Here is how a discussion between a candidate and an interviewer typically unfolds:

Candidate: What model architecture and precision are we sizing for this pretraining run?
Interviewer: We are training a dense model based on the Llama 3.1 8B architecture—32 layers, hidden dimension 4,096, 32 query heads, 8 key-value heads, and a vocabulary size of 128,256, totaling 8.03 billion parameters. All weights, gradients, and activations run in 16-bit brain floating point (BF16).

Candidate: What is the training data volume and sequence context length?
Interviewer: The dataset is 1 trillion tokens (1e12 tokens). Sequences are packed to a context length of 8,192 tokens.

Candidate: What hardware cluster do we have allocated for the job?
Interviewer: We have 256 NVIDIA H100 80 GB SXM GPUs distributed across 32 nodes. Each node contains 8 GPUs interconnected via fourth-generation NVLink at 900 GB/s per GPU. Across nodes, each node connects through 8 InfiniBand NDR NICs offering 400 Gb/s (50 GB/s) per GPU.

Candidate: What is the peak theoretical BF16 throughput of these GPUs?
Interviewer: Each H100 SXM GPU provides 989 TFLOPS of dense BF16 tensor core compute. For financial budgeting, assume cloud capacity costs $3.00 per H100-hour.

Candidate: What target efficiency are we aiming for, and what batch size are we running?
Interviewer: The global batch size is approximately 4 million tokens (512 sequences of 8,192 tokens). We need to achieve at least 40% Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU). We want to know how long the run will take, what the compute will cost, and what happens if our software stack only reaches 30% MFU.

The question underneath is: How do we translate matrix operations and silicon specifications into reliable wall-clock schedules and cloud budgets, and how do we debug the hardware-software gap when throughput falls short? To answer it, we begin by counting the arithmetic operations of a training step.

The 6ND Rule: Counting Training Operations

Matrix multiplication arithmetic

A standard dense transformer consists primarily of linear projection layers: the attention projection matrices (Wq,Wk,Wv,Wo) and the feed-forward network matrices (Wgate,Wup,Wdown in SwiGLU). Each linear transformation computes a General Matrix Multiply (GEMM) of the form Y=XW, where X∈ℝB×din and W∈ℝdin×dout.

For every element in the output matrix Y, computing the dot product between a row of X and a column of W requires din multiplications and (din−1) additions, which is conventionally rounded to 2din floating-point operations. Because the weight matrix contains din×dout parameters, multiplying a batch of B tokens requires exactly:

FLOPs=2×B×din×dout=2×B×Parameters

Summed across all linear layers in the network, a single forward pass requires exactly 2N FLOPs per token, where N is the number of active model parameters [2].

The backward pass: activation gradients and weight gradients

During backpropagation, every linear layer must execute two separate matrix multiplications to propagate derivatives backward through the computational graph [5]:

  1. Gradient with respect to inputs (∂ℒ/∂X): To pass error signals to upstream layers, the layer computes the matrix product of upstream activation gradients ∂ℒ/∂Y and transposed weights W⊤: ∂ℒ∂X=(∂ℒ∂Y)W⊤ Because W⊤ has dimensions dout×din, this operation has the exact same arithmetic dimensions as the forward pass: 2×B×dout×din=2N FLOPs per token.
  2. Gradient with respect to weights (∂ℒ/∂W): To update the layer's weights, the layer computes the outer product of transposed input activations X⊤ and upstream gradients ∂ℒ/∂Y: ∂ℒ∂W=X⊤(∂ℒ∂Y) This GEMM also operates over din×B×dout tensors, requiring another 2×B×din×dout=2N FLOPs per token.

Consequently, the backward pass requires exactly twice the compute of the forward pass (4N FLOPs per token). Combining the forward pass (2N) and backward pass (4N) gives the foundational 6ND rule:

Cdense≈6×N×D

Here Cdense is the total floating-point operations required to train a model with N parameters on D tokens, excluding attention matrices, embedding lookup tables, and layer normalizations [2].

Full activation recomputation: the 8ND rule

As explored in Lesson 3, Training Memory, storing all intermediate forward activations across 32 layers for an 8,192-token context easily triggers out-of-memory errors. The standard mitigation is activation checkpointing (rematerialization). Under full activation checkpointing, only the boundary activations entering each transformer layer are saved during the forward pass; all internal intermediate activations are discarded [3].

When the backward pass reaches that layer, it executes an additional forward pass through the layer to regenerate the discarded activations immediately before backpropagating through them. This extra forward pass requires another 2N FLOPs per token. Under full activation recomputation, the total compute expands to:

Crecompute=2ND (forward)+2ND (recompute)+4ND (backward)=8ND

Full activation recomputation thus imposes an exact 33.3% compute penalty (8ND/6ND=1.333). In exchange, activation memory is slashed by over 90%, allowing larger micro-batches that saturate GPU Tensor Cores [3].

The quadratic attention term and sequence length

The 6ND and 8ND approximations assume that linear projection GEMMs dominate total compute. However, self-attention contains two operations whose compute scales quadratically with sequence length s: computing query-key attention scores (QK⊤) and multiplying attention probabilities by values (PV) [4].

Let s be the context length, h the hidden dimension, and L the number of layers. For a single sequence, projecting Q∈ℝs×h against K⊤∈ℝh×s requires 2s2h FLOPs per layer. Multiplying the resulting s×s attention weights by V∈ℝs×h requires another 2s2h FLOPs per layer. Across L layers, the forward attention pass takes 4Ls2h FLOPs per sequence, which equates to 4Lsh FLOPs per token [5].

The backward pass through attention requires computing gradients with respect to queries, keys, and values, taking 2× the forward compute (8Lsh FLOPs per token). Summing forward and backward yields the exact analytical attention FLOPs per token:

Attention FLOPs per token=12×L×s×h

Adding the attention term to the dense 6N term yields the comprehensive transformer FLOPs equation [4]:

Ctotal=6ND+12LshD=D×(6N+12Lsh)

Figure 2 illustrates how this quadratic term behaves across different sequence lengths for our running Llama 3.1 8B architecture (N=8.03×109,L=32,h=4,096).

At short context lengths (s≤2,048), the attention term accounts for less than 7% of total compute and is safely omitted from rough sizing calculations. However, at our pretraining context length of s=8,192, attention adds 12×32×8,192×4,096≈1.29×1010 FLOPs per token, which represents 26.7% of the dense 6N budget. When models scale to long contexts of 32K or 128K tokens, quadratic attention compute explodes, necessitating FlashAttention-2 [6], sequence parallelism, or context parallelism (see Lesson 12, Tensor, Sequence and Context Parallelism).

Measuring Hardware Efficiency: MFU vs HFU

The PaLM paper definitions

When training an LLM on thousands of GPUs, tracking raw clock time is insufficient. Systems teams must know how close the hardware is running to its physical thermodynamic limits. In 2022, Chowdhery et al. introduced two formal utilization metrics in the PaLM paper to standardize cross-cluster comparisons [1]:

  • Hardware FLOPs Utilization (HFU): The percentage of theoretical peak hardware FLOPS executed by all physical operations occurring on the silicon during training. The numerator counts all floating-point instructions dispatched to tensor cores, including repeated forward passes from activation recomputation, padding tokens, and architectural overhead. HFU=Actual FLOPs Executed per StepPeak Hardware FLOPS×Step Time
  • Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU): The ratio of the theoretical minimum number of FLOPs required to execute the forward and backward passes of the model architecture to the total peak theoretical hardware FLOPs of the cluster over wall-clock time. MFU=6×N×Tokens per StepCluster Peak FLOPS×Step Time=Observed Tokens/sec×6NTotal Cluster Peak FLOPS

Why MFU is the honest metric

The crucial insight is that HFU is implementation-dependent, whereas MFU is implementation-independent [1]. Consider a cluster running at a fixed token throughput. If an engineer enables full activation recomputation, the physical hardware executes 33% more operations per second because tensor cores repeatedly re-evaluate layer forward passes. The reported HFU jumps from 40.0% to 53.3%:

HFU=86×MFU≈1.333×MFU

However, the job does not advance any faster. Not a single extra training token is processed. HFU acts as a vanity metric that artificially rewards software systems for wasting energy on redundant math. MFU, by contrast, holds the numerator strictly to 6ND (or 6ND+12LshD if attention is factored in). If a new algorithmic kernel reduces activation memory without recomputation, MFU stays constant or rises, accurately reflecting true engineering progress [1].

What Eats Your MFU? The Efficiency Tax

If NVIDIA H100 Tensor Cores can execute 989 trillion floating-point operations every second, why do frontier clusters sustain only 400 to 450 TFLOPS of useful work? The 60% loss breaks down into four structural engineering bottlenecks, illustrated as a waterfall in Figure 4.

1. Memory-bandwidth-bound operations

Tensor Cores achieve their advertised peak throughput only during compute-bound dense GEMMs where arithmetic intensity exceeds 150 FLOPs per byte. Transformer architectures contain numerous operators that do not perform matrix multiplications [5]:

  • RMSNorm and LayerNorm: Compute elementwise sums, variances, and scaling. These operations are strictly bound by HBM3 memory bandwidth (3.35 TB/s on H100) and run at less than 5% of Tensor Core compute capability.
  • Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE): Apply 2D rotation matrices to every token query and key vector, requiring elementwise slicing and transcendental trigonometric evaluations.
  • SwiGLU activation functions: Evaluate Swish(xWgate)⊙(xWup), involving elementwise multiplications and sigmoids across 14,336 dimensions.
  • Softmax and dropout: Normalize attention matrices row by row. While FlashAttention fuses these into SRAM, their arithmetic intensity remains fundamentally lower than a GEMM [6].

2. Collective communication overhead

In distributed training, GPUs must constantly exchange gradients, activations, and parameter shards. Across 256 GPUs, synchronizing states via All-Reduce or Reduce-Scatter traverses inter-node InfiniBand networks. Even with 400 Gb/s (50 GB/s) NDR NICs, inter-node network bandwidth is 18 times slower than intra-node NVLink (900 GB/s) and 67 times slower than local HBM (3.35 TB/s). If communication cannot be fully overlapped behind backward GEMMs, GPUs stall in idle synchronization barriers (see Lesson 10, Data Parallelism and All-Reduce) [5].

3. Pipeline bubbles and synchronization barriers

When training models across tens of pipeline stages, GPUs at the end of the pipeline must wait for activations to propagate forward, while GPUs at the beginning must wait for gradients to propagate backward. In a 1F1B (one forward, one backward) schedule with p pipeline stages and m micro-batches, the pipeline bubble fraction is [5]:

Bubble Fraction=p−1m+p−1

If micro-batch depth m is small relative to pipeline stages p, GPUs sit completely idle for 10% to 25% of the step duration (see Lesson 13, Pipeline Parallelism).

4. Data starvation, host overhead, and cluster goodput

If CPU dataloaders cannot tokenize, collate, and transfer 4 million tokens to HBM before the previous step completes, GPUs stall on the CUDA stream. Furthermore, real-world clusters suffer from straggler GPUs with thermal throttling, silent data corruption, optical transceiver packet drops, and checkpoint save stalls. As explored in Lesson 29, Failures, Restarts and Elastic Training, cluster goodput—the fraction of total operational hours spent computing valid forward-backward steps—is frequently 5% to 10% lower than instantaneous MFU.

Tokens per Second: The Operational Metric

Translating MFU into daily telemetry

While MFU is the standard metric for academic papers and capacity proposals, cluster on-call engineers do not read MFU directly from physical sensors. Hardware telemetry dashboards track tokens per second per GPU and aggregate cluster token throughput. Translating between MFU and tokens per second requires a simple inversion of the MFU definition [1]:

Tokens / sec / GPU=MFU×Peak Theoretical FLOPS6×N

Throughput per GPU=0.40×(989×1012)6×(8.03×109)=3.956×10144.818×1010≈8,211 tokens / sec / GPU

Across the entire 256-GPU cluster, the telemetry target is 256×8,211≈2,102,000 tokens / sec (2.10 million tokens/second). If the Grafana dashboard shows 2.10M tokens/s, the cluster is hitting its 40% target.

Which MFU target should we plan for?

When presenting a cluster capacity plan to leadership, teams frequently ask: "Can we plan for 50% MFU, or should we assume 30% to be safe?" Planning for 40% MFU is the engineering sweet spot for dense models on modern H100 clusters running Megatron-LM or PyTorch FSDP2. Planning for 50% requires zero pipeline bubbles, perfect network overlap, and minimal host overhead—conditions rarely sustained outside specialized benchmarks [7].

Conversely, settling for 30% MFU carries a severe financial and operational penalty. Let us recalculate our pretraining run assuming the engineering team fails to overlap communications or misconfigures activation checkpointing, yielding 30% MFU:

  • Tokens / sec / GPU at 30% MFU: (0.30×989×1012)/(6×8.03×109)≈6,158 tokens / sec
  • Total wall-clock time: 4.818×1022/(256×989×1012×0.30)≈6.343×105 s≈176.2 hours≈7.34 days
  • Total GPU-hours consumed: 176.2 hours×256=45,107.3 GPU-hours
  • Cloud rental cost: 45,107.3×3.00 dollars/hour≈135,322 dollars

Figure 6 compares the two outcomes side by side.

The difference between 30% and 40% MFU is not an abstract academic detail: a 10% MFU deficit costs exactly $33,830.47 in wasted cash and delays the training schedule by 1.84 days (44 cluster hours) on a single 1-trillion-token run. Spending two engineering sprints profiling kernels and overlapping communications with collective buckets pays for itself many times over.

def estimate_training_run(
    param_count: float,
    token_count: float,
    num_gpus: int,
    gpu_peak_tflops: float,
    target_mfu: float,
    cost_per_gpu_hour: float = 3.00,
    seq_len: int = 8192,
    num_layers: int = 32,
    hidden_dim: int = 4096,
):
    """
    Computes total training FLOPs, wall-clock duration, GPU-hours,
    financial cost, and target tokens/sec/GPU.
    """
    # 1. Compute FLOPs
    dense_flops = 6.0 * param_count * token_count
    attn_flops_per_token = 12.0 * num_layers * seq_len * hidden_dim
    total_attn_flops = attn_flops_per_token * token_count
    total_flops = dense_flops + total_attn_flops

    # 2. Compute Cluster Speed
    gpu_peak_flops = gpu_peak_tflops * 1e12
    effective_flops_per_gpu = target_mfu * gpu_peak_flops
    cluster_effective_flops = num_gpus * effective_flops_per_gpu

    # 3. Time and Resource Accounting
    wall_clock_sec = dense_flops / cluster_effective_flops
    wall_clock_hours = wall_clock_sec / 3600.0
    wall_clock_days = wall_clock_hours / 24.0
    gpu_hours = wall_clock_hours * num_gpus
    total_cost = gpu_hours * cost_per_gpu_hour

    # 4. Operational Telemetry
    tokens_per_sec_per_gpu = effective_flops_per_gpu / (6.0 * param_count)
    cluster_tokens_per_sec = tokens_per_sec_per_gpu * num_gpus

    return {
        "dense_flops": dense_flops,
        "total_flops_with_attn": total_flops,
        "attn_overhead_pct": (total_attn_flops / dense_flops) * 100.0,
        "wall_clock_days": wall_clock_days,
        "wall_clock_hours": wall_clock_hours,
        "gpu_hours": gpu_hours,
        "total_cost_usd": total_cost,
        "tok_per_sec_per_gpu": tokens_per_sec_per_gpu,
        "cluster_tok_per_sec": cluster_tokens_per_sec,
    }


# Sizing our running pretraining scenario
res = estimate_training_run(
    param_count=8.03e9,
    token_count=1.0e12,
    num_gpus=256,
    gpu_peak_tflops=989.0,
    target_mfu=0.40,
    cost_per_gpu_hour=3.00,
    seq_len=8192,
    num_layers=32,
    hidden_dim=4096,
)

print(f"Dense 6ND FLOPs:       {res['dense_flops']:.2e}")
print(f"Attention Overhead:    {res['attn_overhead_pct']:.1f}%")
print(f"Wall-Clock Time:       {res['wall_clock_days']:.2f} days ({res['wall_clock_hours']:.1f} hrs)")
print(f"Hardware Compute:      {res['gpu_hours']:,.0f} GPU-hours")
print(f"Total Budgeted Cost:   ${res['total_cost_usd']:,.2f}")
print(f"Target Telemetry/GPU:  {res['tok_per_sec_per_gpu']:.1f} tokens/s")
print(f"Target Cluster Speed:  {res['cluster_tok_per_sec']:,.0f} tokens/s")
import time
import torch

class MFUMonitor:
    """
    Tracks step execution time and reports achieved Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU)
    directly inside a PyTorch distributed training loop.
    """
    def __init__(self, num_params: int, peak_flops_per_gpu: float, world_size: int):
        self.num_params = num_params
        self.peak_flops = peak_flops_per_gpu
        self.world_size = world_size
        self.flops_per_token = 6.0 * num_params
        self.start_time = None

    def tic(self):
        # Synchronize CUDA device to record exact step boundary
        if torch.cuda.is_available():
            torch.cuda.synchronize()
        self.start_time = time.perf_counter()

    def toc(self, batch_tokens_per_gpu: int):
        if torch.cuda.is_available():
            torch.cuda.synchronize()
        elapsed = time.perf_counter() - self.start_time

        # Calculate metrics
        tok_per_sec_per_gpu = batch_tokens_per_gpu / elapsed
        achieved_flops_per_gpu = tok_per_sec_per_gpu * self.flops_per_token
        mfu = achieved_flops_per_gpu / self.peak_flops

        return {
            "step_time_ms": elapsed * 1000.0,
            "tok_per_sec_gpu": tok_per_sec_per_gpu,
            "cluster_tok_per_sec": tok_per_sec_per_gpu * self.world_size,
            "mfu": mfu,
        }

# Example instantiation for Llama 3.1 8B on H100 SXM (989 TFLOPS dense BF16)
monitor = MFUMonitor(
    num_params=8_030_000_000,
    peak_flops_per_gpu=989e12,
    world_size=256
)

# Simulated step: micro-batch 2, seq_len 8,192 => 16,384 tokens per GPU per step
tokens_in_step = 16_384
monitor.tic()
# [Execute forward, backward, optimizer step here...]
time.sleep(1.995)  # Simulating a 1.995s step execution
stats = monitor.toc(tokens_in_step)

print(f"Step Time:      {stats['step_time_ms']:.1f} ms")
print(f"Throughput/GPU: {stats['tok_per_sec_gpu']:.1f} tokens/s")
print(f"Cluster Speed:  {stats['cluster_tok_per_sec']:,.0f} tokens/s")
print(f"Achieved MFU:   {stats['mfu'] * 100.0:.2f}%")

Other Talking Points

If there is extra time at the end of the interview, here are some additional talking points:

  • FP8 Training and the MFU Denominator: Modern accelerators like the NVIDIA H100 support FP8 Tensor Core operations with double the theoretical throughput (1,979 TFLOPS vs 989 TFLOPS BF16). When transitioning to FP8, engineers must explicitly specify whether MFU is calculated against the BF16 peak or the FP8 peak [7]. Calculating against BF16 peak can yield apparent MFU values exceeding 70%, which is mathematically invalid; MFU should always divide by the peak theoretical speed of the active arithmetic format.
  • FlashAttention without FLOP Inflation: Standard naive attention materializes an s×s attention score matrix in HBM, generating massive memory traffic. FlashAttention computes exact attention within fast on-chip SRAM via tiling [6]. It dramatically reduces wall-clock time without increasing FLOPs, directly improving observed MFU.
  • Bucket Sizing in Distributed Data Parallelism: PyTorch DistributedDataParallel (DDP) and FSDP2 group parameter gradients into contiguous memory "buckets" (typically 25 MB to 50 MB) to overlap all-reduce communication with backward pass computation. Sub-optimal bucket sizes cause either high latency overhead (buckets too small) or delayed communication overlap (buckets too large), causing MFU to plummet (see Lesson 10, Data Parallelism and All-Reduce).
  • DCGM Profiling and Tensor Core Active Ratio: The NVIDIA Data Center GPU Manager (DCGM) exposes hardware performance counters such as DCGM_FI_PROF_PIPE_TENSOR_ACTIVE. Monitoring this counter reveals what percentage of clock cycles the hardware tensor pipelines are physically executing math, allowing engineers to isolate memory-bound kernel stalls from network communication stalls.
  • Impact of Variable Sequence Lengths and Document Packing: Padding sequences to a fixed context length creates "phantom tokens" that consume forward and backward matrix operations without contributing to learning. Modern training pipelines employ document packing (concatenating multiple documents separated by end-of-text tokens into single 8,192-token sequences) to ensure 100% of computed FLOPs train genuine tokens (see Lesson 17, Pretraining Data Pipelines).
  • Cluster Goodput vs MFU: While MFU measures efficiency during active step execution, cluster goodput factors in node failures, checkpoint serialization overhead, NCCL watchdog timeouts, and pipeline restart drains. In large-scale training across thousands of GPUs, an MFU of 42% combined with 88% goodput results in an effective end-to-end efficiency of 37% [3].

Summary

  • Dense transformer pretraining requires approximately 6ND floating-point operations (2ND for forward pass, 4ND for backward pass). Full activation recomputation adds 2ND, reaching 8ND (+33.3% compute).
  • The quadratic attention term contributes 12Lsh FLOPs per token. For an 8B model, attention represents only 6.7% overhead at 2,048 tokens, but climbs to 26.7% at 8,192 tokens and over 100% at 32K context.
  • Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) measures the ratio of theoretical minimal compute (6ND) to peak hardware capacity over wall-clock time. Unlike Hardware FLOPs Utilization (HFU), MFU is implementation-independent and does not reward redundant recomputations.
  • Frontier dense LLM training runs on H100 clusters typically achieve between 38% and 45% MFU (e.g., Llama 3 405B achieved 41%–43% MFU across up to 16K GPUs). Losses stem from memory-bound non-GEMM operators, inter-node network synchronization, and pipeline bubbles.
  • Daily cluster telemetry translates MFU into tokens/sec/GPU via Tokens/sec/GPU=(MFU×Peak FLOPS)/(6N). For Llama 3.1 8B on H100 at 40% MFU, this target is 8,211 tokens/s/GPU.
  • Our running pretraining run (8.03B model, 1T tokens, 256 H100s at 40% MFU) requires 4.82×1022 FLOPs, taking 5.51 days (33,830 GPU-hours) and costing $101,491. Underperforming at 30% MFU wastes $33,830 and delays the project by 1.84 days.
